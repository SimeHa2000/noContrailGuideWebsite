How to pick the flight with the smallest climate impact

Two tickets for the same trip can differ a lot in how much they warm the planet. Most of that difference comes down to three choices you make when you book: the route, the time of day, and the aircraft.

By Simeon Hatzopoulos · contrail researcher, University of Cambridge
1

Fly direct, and take the train for short hops

Take-off and climb are the most fuel-hungry parts of any flight. A connection adds a second take-off and climb, and usually extra distance, so a one-stop itinerary almost always burns more than the nonstop.

The worst offenders are short feeder flights into a hub, especially within Europe. Stuttgart to Frankfurt for a long-haul connection is a classic example. The ICE train covers it in a little over an hour and stops at Frankfurt Airport itself. Lufthansa even sells it as part of your ticket, with a flight number, through its Express Rail service.

Do thisPick the nonstop whenever one exists. If your first leg is under about 500 km and there's a fast train, take the train to the hub.
2

Fly during the day

Contrails have two effects. They trap heat rising from the ground, and in daylight they also reflect sunlight back to space, which partly cancels the warming. At night only the warming remains. A contrail that forms in the evening and lingers after sunset does the most harm.

25% → 60–80%Night flights are a quarter of daily traffic but cause most of the contrail warming.
22% → 50%Winter flights are a fifth of the year's traffic but cause half of its contrail warming.

Those figures come from a study of flights over the UK (Stuber et al., Nature, 2006). Winter matters because persistent contrails form more often in the cold, humid upper air of the northern winter, and the nights are long.

Do thisChoose a departure that keeps most of your time at cruise altitude in daylight. Avoid red-eyes and evening departures when you can, especially in winter. The flight guide shows this for your route, hour by hour.
3

Fly modern aircraft with two efficient engines

Newer twin-engine aircraft such as the Airbus A320neo and A350, the Boeing 737 MAX and the 787 burn far less fuel per seat than older types, and much less than four-engine jets like the Boeing 747 and Airbus A380. In an analysis of transatlantic airlines by the International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT), the least efficient airline burned 63% more fuel per passenger-kilometre than the most efficient. The main reason was that more than half its flights used 747s and A380s.

This is where low-cost carriers do surprisingly well. They tend to fly young fleets of a single modern type, fit more seats into each aircraft and fill nearly all of them. Fuel per passenger falls on all three counts. Premium cabins work the other way: a business-class seat takes up much more space, and so more fuel, than an economy seat.

Engine choice matters for contrails too. Lean-burn engines such as GE's LEAP, GEnx and GE9X emit far less soot. Less soot means fewer, larger ice crystals, so the contrail is thinner and fades sooner.

Do thisCheck the aircraft type before you book. Prefer a recent twin-engine type, fly economy, and don't rule out a budget airline.
Going further

The first three rules cover most of the difference. These two are worth doing too, but they come with caveats.

4

Fly with airlines trialling contrail avoidance

Some airlines and air traffic controllers now make small altitude changes to steer flights around the patches of cold, humid air where persistent contrails form. Cathay Pacific runs trials with Google across Asia and the Pacific. Operation Blue Skies, led by Google with NATS and the UK government, starts adjusting flights over the North Atlantic in winter 2026–27. In an airline trial in the US, flights that followed the avoidance plan formed 62% fewer contrails, with no measurable extra fuel burn.

The caveat: trials cover only some flights, so booking with a participating airline doesn't guarantee your flight will be rerouted. Choosing those airlines still supports the work and helps it scale.

Do thisWhen two options are otherwise equal, pick the airline that is doing contrail avoidance.
5

Pay extra to compensate for what's left

Once you've chosen the cleanest flight, you can pay to compensate for the emissions that remain. This comes last because compensation can't undo what has already been emitted, and the quality of schemes varies a lot.

Two things to look for. First, pick a scheme with independent certification, such as Gold Standard, or one that funds sustainable aviation fuel directly. Second, check whether the calculator includes the non-CO₂ effects such as contrails. Many count only CO₂, which can leave out a large part of a flight's warming.

Do thisAdd certified compensation to your booking, using a calculator that accounts for non-CO₂ effects.
