Aircraft: what actually matters

  • 1

    Combustor beats age. Lean-burn combustors (GE's TAPS family on the GEnx, LEAP and GE9X) emit far less soot. Fewer soot particles mean fewer, larger ice crystals, so the contrail is optically thinner and falls out sooner.

  • 2

    Efficient engines form contrails slightly more easily. Higher propulsive efficiency means cooler exhaust, which raises the threshold temperature for formation. The soot effect usually matters more for how long the contrail lasts.

  • 3

    Check the engine, not just the airframe. A Boeing 787 can have GEnx (lean-burn) or Trent 1000 (rich-burn) engines depending on the airline. An A320neo can have LEAP-1A or PW1100G. Seat-map sites usually list the variant.

  • 4

    Never trade a nonstop for a connection. The extra take-off and distance add CO₂ that usually outweighs any contrail saving.

Airlines and airspace trialling avoidance

Trials cover a subset of flights, so booking with a trial airline does not mean your flight will be rerouted. The card for your route is highlighted.

North Atlantic · winter 2026–27 onward

Operation Blue Skies

NATS will make small altitude changes for flights crossing Shanwick oceanic airspace northwest of Ireland, guided by Google's forecasts, with the Met Office. Cambridge and Imperial verify the results independently. Participating airlines haven't been named.

Imperial College announcement
Asia & transpacific · 2026

Cathay Pacific with Google

More than 80 of over 100 trial flights followed avoidance routes, with an estimated 40% cut in contrail warming on those flights. A larger second phase is under way.

Google announcement
United States · 2023 pilot

American Airlines with Google and Breakthrough Energy

An early pilot in which pilots used contrail forecasts to change altitude. Later Google analysis of a larger airline-led trial found 62% fewer contrails on flights that flew the avoidance plan, with no measurable fuel penalty.

Google Research paper
Gulf & long-haul

Etihad with SATAVIA

Etihad has run contrail-avoidance flights using SATAVIA's forecasting platform. Check the airline's current sustainability pages for whether it is still active.

How this is calculated. The flight follows the great-circle route at an average 840 km/h, plus 30 minutes for taxi and climb. Winds are ignored, so eastbound transatlantic flights arrive earlier than shown. Cruise starts 25 minutes after take-off and ends 30 minutes before landing. Every 5 minutes of cruise, the sun's elevation is computed at the aircraft's position. A sample counts as night if the sun is below the horizon, and as "lives into the night" if the sun will set there within two hours.

Persistent contrails only form in ice-supersaturated air, which can't be forecast weeks ahead. This guide shows when a contrail would do the most harm, not whether one will form. Nothing here is a booking recommendation.
